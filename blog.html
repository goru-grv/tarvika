Research practice · 6 min read

A good technology project begins with a better question.

A compelling technology idea can make us want to build immediately. But the strongest first step is often to understand the situation well enough to ask what actually needs to change.

Start by describing the context in ordinary language. Who encounters the problem? When does it happen? What do people do today? Which constraints—cost, connectivity, time, language, safety or access—shape the experience? Listening for these details helps distinguish the visible symptom from the underlying need.

Turn an idea into something you can investigate

A useful project question is focused enough to explore and open enough to learn from. Instead of asking “Can AI improve education?”, consider a bounded question such as “What makes it difficult for a particular group of learners to find the next relevant practice exercise?” The narrower question does not prescribe a solution. It creates space to examine evidence before choosing one.

Then decide what would count as a useful observation, which information you are permitted to collect, and whose perspective is missing. If people, personal information or sensitive settings are involved, confirm consent, privacy requirements and any institutional review before starting.

Let evidence change the plan

Once you have a question, choose the least complicated method that can help answer it. A conversation, a review of existing documentation, a small prototype or a carefully scoped test may be more appropriate than a large system. Note the limitations and what remains unknown. A prototype demonstrates that something can be made; it does not, by itself, show that it solves the problem.

When a team can explain the context, the question, the evidence and the limits of its work, technology becomes a considered response rather than the starting assumption.

Emerging technology · 5 min read

Start small when exploring an AI idea.

A small experiment is a practical way to learn where an AI approach might help—and where it might not.

Begin with a task, not a trend. Describe what input the system would receive, what output it would provide, who would use that output and what happens if it is wrong. This makes assumptions visible before a model or dataset enters the picture.

For an early exploration, work with public or synthetic examples when possible. If real people's information is involved, do not collect or reuse it without the appropriate permission and safeguards. Keep a simple record of the data's source, known gaps and any transformations. Data quality and representation can shape results as much as the model does.

Evaluate the part that matters

Choose a baseline and a measure connected to the actual task. Look at examples where the system succeeds and fails, and consider whether those errors affect groups differently. A single summary score can hide important variation. Compare the system's output with a simpler alternative and with the judgment of the people who understand the context.

Finally, state clearly what your experiment cannot establish. A classroom prototype is not evidence of production readiness, and model output is not automatically a fact. Responsible exploration treats uncertainty as something to document, not something to hide.

Learning by doing · 4 min read

Keep a project notebook, not just a finished demo.

A polished result is useful to show. A clear record of how you got there is what helps others understand—and helps you keep learning.

For each work session, capture the question you were trying to answer, the change you made, what you observed and what you plan to try next. Include the tools or versions that matter, links to public references and the reason behind a decision. Small notes are easier to maintain than a retrospective written from memory.

Make uncertainty visible

Save a brief note about a failed approach and why you moved on. Keep track of assumptions that still need checking, known limitations and any feedback that changed your direction. If you work with a team, agree where the record lives and avoid including passwords, private keys, personal information or confidential material.

When you share the project, pair the demonstration with a short explanation: the need, your contribution, your choices, what the evidence shows and what it does not show. That is a stronger learning artifact than a claim that the project is “complete.”

Agri Tech · 5 min read

In Agri Tech, start with the field—not the sensor.

A sensor can produce a measurement. Whether that measurement is useful depends on the question, the place and the people who may act on it.

Before selecting a device, learn how a decision is currently made and what information is already available. Talk with people who understand the local crop, soil, water, season and working conditions. Connectivity, power, maintenance, calibration and cost can determine whether a technically impressive device fits day-to-day practice.

Design a careful, bounded trial

Write down what you intend to measure, where and when measurements will happen, and how you will check whether the readings are plausible. Record missing values and environmental conditions rather than quietly discarding inconvenient results. Compare the sensor with an appropriate reference when possible, and keep a log of calibration and maintenance.

Most importantly, separate measurement from recommendation. A reading is not automatically a diagnosis or an instruction. Share uncertainty in plain language and involve the people who will use the information when deciding whether a tool is helpful. A modest trial with clear limits can reveal what to improve before anyone depends on it.
